**In one line:** the passport only entered the map when the sample was already for sale, almost a year after the supplier incident.

## What happened

On October 2, 2026, La Jornada reproduced a statement sent by Aeroméxico to customers. On September 18 the cybersecurity team saw on Telegram an offer of a base attributed to customers; SABG also reported the case. The internal investigation linked the material to a 2025 incident on an external supplier’s information management platform, in an international campaign. The company confirmed as exposed the passport number, frequent flyer number, full name and date of birth. Nationality, gender, email, phone and preferred route were treated as potentially involved. Financial data, cards and loyalty program passwords, according to the company, were not affected. Operations did not stop.

## Why it matters

A travel document cannot be changed. The gap between the 2025 event and the October 2026 letter shows an incomplete third-party inventory: the public trigger was the offer, not the moment the data left.

## Risk reading

| Vector | Public evidence | Severity |
|---|---|---|
| Information management supplier | Company links the base to a 2025 incident on a third-party platform | High |
| Travel document | Statement cites passport number among the exposed | High |
| Financial data | Company says card, account and loyalty password were not affected | Indeterminate |

## What to do this week

1. List processors that still hold travel documents from prior incidents — privacy and third party.
2. Cross the public sample, if any, with the record the contract required the supplier to retain — security.
3. Sign it worked: every passport field has an owner, retention period and evidence of deletion or exposure.
