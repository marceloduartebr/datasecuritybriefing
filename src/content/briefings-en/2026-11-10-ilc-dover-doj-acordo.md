In one line: The US$ 13 million ILC Dover settlement shows that declared compliance without evidence is already a liability.

## What happened

On November 3, 2026, the U.S. Department of Justice announced that ILC Dover and ILC Astrospace will pay US$ 13 million to resolve False Claims Act allegations. According to the DOJ, between 2018 and 2024 the companies failed to meet cybersecurity requirements in contracts with the Army, Missile Defense Agency and NASA, including storing controlled unclassified information in a third-party cloud without FedRAMP Moderate equivalence and not implementing NIST SP 800-171 and 800-53 controls. The case started with a tip from the former cybersecurity director.

## Why it matters

Enforcement did not depend on a breach: it was enough to show that promised controls did not exist and that the data was outside the required environment. For companies that sign security questionnaires and data-protection clauses, the question stops being "do we have the policy?" and becomes "can we prove where the data is and what protects it?".

## Risk reading

| Vector | Public evidence | Severity |
|---|---|---|
| Controlled data in non-approved cloud | DOJ announcement, November 3 | High |
| NIST controls required by contract not implemented | DOJ announcement | High |
| Internal tip as origin of the case | DOJ announcement | Medium |

## What to do this week

1. List contracts with security requirements and cross-check where the covered data actually is, including non-approved cloud services — owner: compliance and security.
2. Gather technical evidence (not just policy) for every control declared in customer questionnaires — owner: GRC.
3. Signal it worked: for any signed security clause, the company can produce the evidence in one day.
